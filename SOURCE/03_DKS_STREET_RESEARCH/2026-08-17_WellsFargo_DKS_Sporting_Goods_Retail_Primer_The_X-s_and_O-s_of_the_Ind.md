Our Call
In today's ~60 page Primer, we dig deep into Sporting Goods. This includes underlying 
drivers, market composition and growth outlook on what is now a consolidated/more 
profitable subsector to operate in. We likewise u/g DKS to OW (sidelined ASO).
 
Sporting Goods Playbook. The US Sporting Goods industry has structurally changed 
over the last two decades - consolidating with differentiated strategies, shifting into 
higher-margin softlines, developing formidable omni-channel capabilities and solidifying 
winners as critical components of the value chain with key athletic brands. In this report, 
we outline the overall industry trends and dig into the reasons we grow more positive on
DKS (Upgrade to OW) and why we remain sidelined on ASO.
 
Industry Primer. Demand drivers are robust: 1) Daily sports/exercise participation of 23% 
is an all-time high, 2) Youth participation has recovered to 58% (from 49% in 2021) and
3) a record 60%+ of people in the US participate in outdoor recreation. The market is 
growing +LSD and consolidating (led by DKS) while NT growth rates continue to come in 
above historical norms. Mix has shifted to softlines, brands and omni-channel - fending 
off AMZN where share has remained at ~13% for several years.
 
More Positive on DKS. Core DSG is the category leader (w/ sustainable +MSD comps) 
and the business has structurally improved: GMs ~700bps and OM ~600bps above pre-
COVID, sales/sq ft up ~50% while we view the current 11-12% OM as a healthy baseline. 
We see +MSD sales growth on +LSD underlying comp (ex-relocations and GameChanger), 
with House of Sport and Field House remodels earning high 4-wall ROIC and adding 
+200-275bps to comp. As DSG keeps investing behind the model, their moat grows 
larger.
 
Foot Locker Remains Key Swing Factor. We estimate Nike's allocation reset drove 
at least ~1,000bps of sales pressure and ~490bps of the ~880bps OM decline from 
FY21-FY23 - an issue now reversing, particularly with DSG's merchandising and vendor 
playbook. Based on our deep analysis, from ~1-2% today we see a path for FL to recapture 
300-400bps of lost MM while adding 200-300bps of leverage (assuming +LSD comp 
growth) - which puts a 7-8% margin on the table for FY28 (well above St's ~5.0%).
 
Remain Sidelined on ASO. Inexpensive, but unproven. Structurally, it's stuck in the middle 
- later access to brands, e-commerce at 12% (vs. DKS 15%+) and a store base skewed 
to lower-income/lower-participation states. That said, the business is making strides 
in working with brands, developing its omni-channel capabilities, and attracting higher-
income consumers while refining its square footage expansion strategy. We need a 
durable comp inflection to shift our views.

Equity Research
Executive Summary
Overview. The US sporting goods industry has changed structurally over the last two decades. What 
was once an equipment business defined by hardgoods is now a $150-250B US market (depending 
on definition), built on softlines, brand access and omni-channel execution - supported by a 
consumer that has durably re-rated spending on health, fitness and the outdoors. The category 
has consolidated — organically and through M&A — and the surviving scale players have built genuine 
moats: fitting, service, team-sports relationships, vendor partnerships and immediate availability lead
the key drivers that have held Amazon's category share flat at ~13% for the better part of a decade.
Against that backdrop, we are upgrading DKS to Overweight (PT to $240, ~14.5x our FY27E EPS 
of $16.50). We see a core DICK's business operating from a structurally higher base, an under-
appreciated set of initiatives compounding on each other and a Foot Locker turnaround the market is 
underwriting too conservatively — a combination we view as asymmetric at current levels. We remain 
Equal Weight on ASO (PT $50), where valuation is undemanding but comp durability, brand access and 
falling returns keep us sidelined.
Key Highlights in Today's Report:
1) Industry Primer. The demand backdrop is as healthy as it has been in two decades. Daily 
participation in sports and exercise is at an all-time high 23% of the US population, annual participation 
is ~75%, and inactivity has fallen for seven consecutive years. Youth participation has recovered 
to 58% of children ages 6-17 (from 49% in 2021) and a record 181M Americans (over 60% of the 
population age six and older) took part in outdoor recreation in 2024 (+3% YoY), with the fastest 
growth in higher-income and older cohorts. The market itself is $150-250B and grows +LSD, but the 
mix has changed: DKS is now 76% softlines pro forma with nearly half the business in footwear, brand 
access has become the primary point of differentiation and omni-channel has moved from cost center 
to moat. Macro indicators are inflecting — category PCE has grown +5% annually for 20 years and 
accelerated to roughly +12% in June, while retail sales are +7% 2026 YTD against a +3% long-term 
average.
2) We Are Positive on DKS. Today, we upgrade DKS to OW. Core DSG is the strongest asset in the 
category. GM is ~700bps and operating margin ~600bps above pre-COVID, sales psf are up nearly 
+50%, and we view the 11-12% operating margin as the new baseline rather than a peak. Underlying 
comp of +1.0-1.5% (ex-relocations and GameChanger) is more modest than the reported +4-5%, but 
House of Sport and Field House remodels are earning 27% and 42% 4-wall ROIC at 120-125% of the 
productivity of the stores they replace, and the initiative set — GameChanger, paid loyalty and DICK's 
Media Network — is more strategically linked than the market credits. Notably, Foot Locker is the 
swing factor and the source of the upside: Nike's allocation reset drove ~1,000bps of sales pressure 
and ~490bps of the ~880bps operating margin decline from FY21 to FY23, an issue now reversing 
under DSG's merchandising playbook. FL earned ~9% OM and 30%+ GM before COVID; consensus 
embeds only 5% by FY28, so we do not need a home run.
3) We Remain Equal Weight on ASO. ASO is inexpensive (just 7-8x P/E), but we need evidence before 
getting constructive. Comps have been negative every year since FY22 and the recent inflection is 
ticket-led — 1Q26's +2.9% comp was entirely price (+4.5% ticket against -1.5% transactions), which 
is harder to sustain if the low-end consumer stays pressured. Structurally, the business is stuck in 
the middle: not specialized enough for the enthusiast, not cheap enough for the mass shopper - with 
later access to brands than DKS, e-commerce under penetrated and a store base weighted to states 
with lower income/lower youth-sports participation than the US average. Productivity and returns 
keep eroding — store sales per square foot of $252 versus $276 in FY17, and lease-adjusted ROIC 
down every year since FY21 to 10.1%. The offsets are real and keep us at Equal Weight rather than 
Underweight: the refined outside-in store strategy should add ~100bps to comp, gross margin has 
held at 34-35%, and retail media, loyalty and better brand allocation offer margin support.

Dick's Sporting Goods (DKS) Summary
We Are More Positive on DKS. We Upgrade shares of DKS to Overweight: (Upgrade To OW; LT 
Upside Outweighs NT Noise, PT to $240). The three main reasons we are positive on DKS: 1) FL 
can drive meaningful upside to estimates as DKS is bringing highly complementary operational 
and merchandising capabilities that directly address the historic weaknesses at the FL business, re-
establishing healthier brand relationships and allocations that have driven at least half of the earnings 
deterioration over the last 4 years, which we think should clear relatively low expectations for margin 
recovery and drive meaningful EPS upside; 2) The core DICK's Sporting Goods business remains 
attractive as the category leader, delivering +LSD/+MSD growth with a highly defensible moat in 
omni-channel capabilities, merchandising and partnerships with brands that have driven sustainable 
improvements in store productivity, margins, and returns; and 3) The more work we have done on the 
multiple initiatives at DKS (expanding the fleet of stores with unique concepts, the expansion into paid 
loyalty, the GameChanger app, and DICK's Media Network), the more compelled we are that these are 
working together in driving value and engagement with the highest-value customers, breaking apart 
from competition, and further cementing its position as the partner of choice in the US for sporting 
goods and athletic brands.
There are considerable risks to this view, and the story has no doubt become increasingly complex 
to analyze - but therein lies the opportunity. We believe the most critical points of contention are: 1)
Continued skepticism on FL with both structural concerns on the need to rationalize the store base 
and pressure on lower-income consumers, as well as near-term concerns on the durability of Fast 
Break initiatives into 2H26, especially as Nike speaks to deteriorating sell-through at wholesale and 
ongoing weakness in lifestyle athletic footwear; 2) Concerns on the core DSG business which has 
become increasingly difficult to dissect as the strategy evolved, reporting methodologies changed, and 
investments into the unproven initiatives hold back earnings flow-through on otherwise strong sales 
growth; and 3) Risks to the combined entity given a history of failed M&A in scaled US retail, added 
complexity from incorporating an international business, and a much higher concentration to footwear 
as a category and to Nike.
While the stock has recently retrenched on concerns that the overall business is slowing (especially 
footwear) following the World Cup in the US, we believe the pullback presents a more compelling 
opportunity for investors with longer-duration that want exposure to the NA athletic market without 
being fully exposed to the rise and fall of individual brands. Our PT of $240 is based on 14.5x applied to 
our CY27 EPS estimate of $16.50.

Top Three Reasons We Are Positive DKS
1) Foot Locker Recovery Can Drive Meaningful Upside. In our view, getting comfortable that FL 
is fixable is the gating item for many investors today. In our work, we come away incrementally 
more positive that there is potential upside, and a relatively modest lift can deliver meaningful EPS 
upside. 1) The strategic rationale for buying FL made sense, bringing DSG's leading omni-channel and 
merchandising capabilities and brand relationships to FL addresses FL's biggest weaknesses - this is 
evidenced by the early results at the Fast Break initiatives comping +DD with improving merchandise 
margin; 2) Nike's reduction in wholesale allocation at FL created a nearly ~1,000bps impact to sales, 
and we estimate a nearly ~490bps impact to OM (over half of the deterioration alone) - this issue is 
clearly reversing; 3) While still difficult to reconcile whether Nike can return to healthy brand growth 
in NA, especially given commentary on deteriorating sell-through trends starting in April of this year, 
we are more comfortable that the trajectory is at least improving from trough levels; and 4) We don't 
need a home run at FL to drive meaningful upside to DKS estimates - and a return of OM to > 7% (still 
below pre-COVID levels) has the potential to drive DKS EPS north of $20.00 (Street $18.69) in 
FY28, all else equal.
2) Core Dick's Sporting Goods Remains an Attractive Business. We still like the core DSG business 
as the established category leader for all things sport. While their success today is rarely a topic of 
debate among investors, we believe the health of the business today is highly durable and built on 
nearly a decade of work. 1) We see a +LSD underlying comp at DSG coupled with the continued 
expansion of House of Sport and Field House relocations and renovations delivering attractive +LSD/
+MSD sales growth with both ticket and traffic; 2) Omni-channel capabilities built from in-housing 
capabilities starting back in 2017 remain unmatched in the space and will be difficult to replicate, 
driving market share while protecting margins; 3) Developed partnership with brands has structurally 
shifted the balance of power in DSG's favor, ensuring it remains the partner of choice for brands 
while they are still in the attractive part of their lifecycle and selling full-price; 4) Store productivity 
metrics are still trending positively, driving stronger occupancy leverage over the long-term while 
enabling reinvestment behind SG&A; and 5) Margins that are comfortably in the 11%-12% range as a 
result of the above, with underlying improvements allowing for continued reinvestment to expand its 
competitive moat (Fun Fact: DKS's CapEx guide of ~$1B for the DSG banner is nearly 5x the $200M-
$240M guided by ASO for this year, over 300bps higher as a % of segment sales).
3) An Abundance of Opportunities Working in Unison. While the multiple initiatives at DSG may 
seem independent of each other, we believe they all have the makings for a very interesting flywheel 
and are more strategically intertwined than the company gets credit for. This includes: 1) a unique 
set of store concepts that each serve a distinct purpose in the overall portfolio (House of Sport, Field 
House, Going, Going, Gone, and Golf Galaxy), 2) direct access to the customers involved in youth 
sports that spend the most in the category through GameChanger; 3) a growing loyalty program and 
potential for recurring subscription revenue; and 4) DICK's Media Network that is well positioned to 
attract more investment from brands (highly profitable "advertising" revenue). These initiatives are 
still early, but they are all building towards the same goals: driving sales with athletes (customers), 
expanding the competitive moat, and making DSG an indispensable partner in the value chain for 
vendors.
We see a reasonable Bull Case scenario for $20.00+ in FY28 EPS
With continued execution on a stable DSG business, a bull-case scenario at FL referenced below, and 
conservative estimates for debt paydown and share repurchases that also assume no change in the tax 
rate, we believe there is a pathway for a Bull case of $20.00+ EPS for FY28 (Exhibit 1 and Exhibit 2). 
Key assumptions underpinning the Bull case are below (which are not inclusive of the 53rd week).
1) Stable DSG. Our DSG estimates embed +4% reported comp (including underlying core +100bps, 
House of Sport/Field House Relocations/Conversions of +270bps and GameChanger +30bps) with 30bps 
of OM improvement (arguably conservative given buying synergies not embedded).
2) FL Margin Recapture. Our FL estimates (detailed below) are built to a ~7.5% OM business with 
improvements from improved product allocation, better merchandising and occupancy/personnel 
leverage with a +3% comp and square footage reductions embedded.
3) Debt Paydown and Share Repurchases. We assume $1B of debt is paid down and $1.2B of share 
repurchases from FY26-FY28, well within the FCF of the business over the period (average ~$1B/yr in 
our base case).

Foot Locker Recovery Can Drive Meaningful Upside
In May 2025, DKS announced its acquisition of FL with expectations to be accretive to EPS in the first 
full FY post-close (FY26) and deliver $100-$125M of cost synergies in the medium-term, and has 
very quickly moved to clear out unproductive inventory and make investments into re-assorted stores 
that are delivering positive results in a relatively quick time. The recovery at FL is a fairly binary event - 
either the problems with FL were mostly attributable to poor merchandising and vendor relationships, 
in which case it is now in capable hands that can quickly turn it around, or the problem is structural, in 
which case this might be a multi-year fix for DKS that will both cost more, drag on overall results and 
distract from the core DSG opportunity.
Our view is the truth will wind up somewhere in-between, but to us, we see it as more likely 
that FL improves meaningfully off a multi-decade low level of earnings - which is the right bet 
at current levels (just ~12x P/E today). Nike is clearly the big point of contention for investors 
today, as it historically has been a significant driver of both growth and margin to the FL business, 
and while we believe the allocation problem that started in 2022-2023 is largely resolved (which 
independently should be a lift to the FL business), the current trajectory of the brand with negative 
DTC and deteriorating sell-through trends remains a big question mark today. That said, we see the 
range of potential outcomes as skewed to the upside from here, as current estimates only embed FL 
returning to approximately half its historic margin levels.
A Brief Summary of the FL Acquisition. The acquisition of FL was announced in May 2025, with 
a consideration of $24/share (a ~100% premium to the trailing 30-day avg. weighted price), 
representing a $2.5B EV (or ~6.4x trailing EV/EBITDA). At the time of the acquisition, DKS discussed 
intentions for FL to be accretive to EPS in the first full fiscal year post-close (FY26) and to deliver 
$100M-$125M of cost synergies in the medium term. The rationale at the time included: 1) create 
a global platform (DSG had never had an international business, and UK based JD acquired a more 
significant US presence with Hibbett in 2024); 2) broaden the set of consumers across concepts (DSG's 
core customer remains different from FL's more urban store base, and younger male sneaker enthusiast);
3) strengthen the relationship with brand partners (DSG's core competency is in merchandising and 
vendor partnerships, an area that was lacking at FL); 4) invest in growth through an industry-leading 
omni-channel experience (sharing DSG's well-established omni-channel capabilities and Retail 101 with 
FL); and 5) unlock operational efficiencies (better procurement and direct sourcing efficiencies, but this 
acquisition also made DKS a larger buyer further tilting the scales in their favor). The acquisition closed in 
September 2025, with 92.6% of FL shareholders electing to receive DKS shares instead of cash.
DKS moved very quickly to purge inventory and implement a re-assorted store program to best 
position the business for 2026. On its 3Q25 earnings call in November, DKS began its "Clean Out 
The Garage" initiative at FL, purging unproductive inventory, and announced its 11-store pilot "Fast 
Break" initiative. By its 4Q25 earnings call in March 2026, DKS indicated that its Fast Break stores 
drove strong positive comps, and it was expanding to 250 locations (primarily US) by back-to-school, 
and that they were expecting to close fewer stores than originally anticipated. Interestingly, DKS's Net 
Capex guide for the year of $1.4B included ~$400M related to FL (>5% of segment sales) related to 
this initiative, and is well ahead of any time in FL's 20-year history averaging ~3% of sales. By 1Q26, 
DKS had completed approximately 100 Fast Break stores, and indicated that these stores were 
comping positive +DD with a merchandise margin improvement. This has become a key datapoint for 
the Bull case: even with a short time frame and a relatively low-touch, DKS has been able to make 
meaningful improvement to the underlying FL business, and this will only get better as the year 
goes on, and new merchandise flows into the store.
A Historical Analysis of Nike's Importance to Foot Locker. Aside from the obvious that Nike 
represents over half of FL's business, we would highlight its importance from a historical perspective. 
Over the last almost two decades, Nike went from 56% of the business in 2007 to 75% of the business

What Happened Over the Last 5 Years? There are two distinct factors that contributed to FL 
struggles with Nike: 1) The initial decision by Nike to reduce allocation to FL (2021-2023) and 2)
The subsequent deterioration of Nike's business in North America necessitating a massive reset of 
supply and reversion back to wholesale (2024-Today). The first factor is clearly reversed or at least 
not a headwind today, as Nike has committed to a more holistic view of the marketplace including its 
partners, though we doubt the penetration at FL drifts much higher than 60% due to diversification 
efforts (it isn't a good thing to be too dependent on one brand). The second factor leads to the big two 
questions today (which we address below in the key risks to DKS) - 1) can Nike return to generating 
growth in NA, and 2) if Nike can't, then can FL still work by growing with other brands through better 
presentation and merchandising (where DKS management excels).
The High Heat Shoe Dropped. On FL's 4Q21 earnings call February 22, 2022, FL announced that 
Nike's accelerated strategic shift to DTC would result in Nike's mix of sales declining by 10 percentage 
points (from 70% to 60%), which equated to approximately $900M of sales, beginning in the 4Q22. 
Fast-forward to the end of 2023, which had a full year of the reduced allocation, and Nike's penetration 
at FL was still 65%, representing a sales decline of approximately $800M from 2021. This mirrored 
the decline in the overall FL business, which also declined by approximately $800M. Our industry 
checks indicate that this was primarily driven by reducing the allocation of high-heat product in 
half, partially offset by infilling other Nike product.
Why Was High-Heat Important? High-heat products were a critical component of Foot Locker's 
business. 1) they drove sales by a) selling at a higher price point and b) driving traffic both online and 
in-store; 2) they had a higher merchandise margin business that a) was rarely marked-down and b) was 
almost always marked for Return-To-Vendor (RTV) when inventory was leftover, and 3) the sales drove 
leverage on both occupancy and SG&A, and the loss led to significant deterioration of both.
Our checks within the industry further point to the run-off impacts of Nike dislodging FL from the 
distribution chain - the loss of "high-heat" or "launch/atomic" product. Before the change in strategies 
at Nike, FL was typically allocated ~40% of "high heat product" (if Nike was selling 1 million pairs, FL 
would get 400k), and this was cut in half to ~20%. An even more exclusive part of this was "launch" 
or "atomic", which FL had an approximate 7-8% allocation from Nike. The high-heat product would 
generally sell through at full price, and if marked-down, it would typically be a LSD amount (vs. the rest 
of the business around high-teens), and the "launch" product would generally completely sell through 
(and drive overall traffic).
The Initial Shock Explains Over Half the Problem. Our analysis attempting to isolate the initial impact 
suggests that the loss of high-heat product from Nike contributed headwinds of approximately 
1,000bps to sales, ~175bps to markdown, ~90bps to occupancy, and ~225bps to SG&A deleverage, 
or approximately 490bps of the 880bps of operating margin deterioration from FY21 to FY23
(Exhibit 8). While there clearly were other factors at play (an additional 100bps of freight pressure 
and merchandise margin pressure from overall promotions on excess inventory), we believe it is 
directionally accurate that the loss of Nike high-heat contributed to almost all the sales headwinds 
and at least half of the margin deterioration. Some key assumptions below: 1) Assume all the Nike 
reduction was "high-heat" being cut in half, 2) assume these products carried a 0% markdown rate 
(vs. our checks that indicated that every other business carried an average high-teens); and 3) if we 
attribute the majority of the sales decline to the Nike change, then the overall occupancy and SG&A 
deleverage is similar.

We are comfortable with the trajectory of the underlying DSG business, with attractive growth and

rebased higher to the 11-12% range as a result of the above, with underlying improvements allowing 
for continued reinvestment and market share opportunities.

Top Three Risks to Our Call
1) Foot Locker Skepticism. The Foot Locker business is likely to draw continued investor scrutiny, 
regardless of how 2H26 performs, due to lingering concerns that it still has long-term structural 
problems that will take longer to address (or if they ever can be). 1) FL business is still over-stored

may not have a fit in the strategy (Champs and WSS); 2) The Fast Break initiatives and trends are 
likely unsustainable, benefiting from inventory write-downs and heavily discounted product as well 
as consumer stimulus; and 3) Nike remains pressured in NA, indicating a slow-down in sell-through 
activity in April, which adds risk to the outlook for a 2H inflection in sales and margin driven by better 
merchandising and allocation.
2) Concerns on Core DSG Business. While management has clearly executed against its strategies,

reclassification of stores that draws continued cynicism from investors, and points to underlying 
comparable sales trends that are less robust than presented (we break out our math for this below); 
and 2) Flow-through on earnings is not as robust as some would hope, may get worse as the level of 
investments accelerates, and larger stores add deleverage risk in a potential slow-down.
3) Threading the Needle on M&A. The primary concerns for the combined entity are: 1) scaled 
M&A in US retail rarely works and may become a distraction for the DKS management team; 2) the

both footwear and Nike. While these are all real risks, we see offsetting factors to each: 1) DKS bought

retail, and the category is more concentrated and less prone to mass competition; 2) the international 
exposure could provide an easy way to test the waters of eventually bringing DSG to other markets, 
while also being able to serve brands globally; and 3) the higher concentration may tilt the balance of 
power between retailer and brand further in DKS's favor, and Nike clearly needs DKS's help today more 
than ever in building and maintaining a healthy US marketplace, which can result in the absolute best 
allocation of product and stronger vendor allowances.

Foot Locker Skepticism
Foot Locker remains the area of the DKS story that faces the most investor skepticism stemming 
from doubts on both the long-term structural integrity of the business and the near-term trends. 1)
FL still has significant exposure to unhealthy mall locations, which may necessitate a larger reset of the 
business; 2) the near-term trends from the early Fast Break initiatives are unsustainable, benefitting 
from a large inventory write-down taken 4Q25 coupled with a consumer that benefited from stimulus; 
and 3) 2H26 outlook for significant margin expansion driven by better allocation of product requires 
a significant leap of faith - even if FL gets better product, the Nike brand is still under pressure with 
negative DTC growth and deteriorating sell-through in its wholesale channels.
Store Base May Need a Larger Reset. FL's store base may require a larger reset than is currently 
contemplated. While FL's prior management has closed hundreds of underperforming locations and 
repositioned the fleet through its 2023 "Lace Up" strategy, a meaningful portion of the chain remains 
tied to secondary malls and lower-traffic shopping centers that continue to face secular pressure from 
declining foot traffic and tenant productivity. As of last disclosed on FL's 2023 Analyst Day, there were 
~850 NA stores in A/B mall locations, and over 450 in C/D mall locations in FY22, with a plan to lower 
this to ~630 A/B and under 250 C/D, while further shifting to off-mall locations. Looking at the FL 
store base and what we think DKS is trying to do with the brand, Champs and WSS might be the most 
likely candidates for further pruning over the medium-term. As of 1Q26, there were 364 Champs 
stores and 100 WSS stores, or ~20% of the total 2,227 stores at FL.
Conversely, there may be opportunity that further store pruning could help drive asset productivity 
from overlapping locations. The overlap between banners is very high in some cases, (over 75% of 
Champs stores are within a mile of a FL store and often in the same mall, which is over 40% of FL 
stores in the US). While the risk is that even if FL may have a smaller sales base, the potential upside 
on revenue recapture is high (i.e., funneling some % of sales to one store in the mall instead of two), 
driving improved store productivity longer-term.
Near-Term Trends Had Discrete Benefits. Shortly after the acquisition closed, DKS undertook the 
"Clean Out The Garage" strategy at FL, clearing through unproductive inventory at FL in preparation 
for 2026. During 4Q25, DKS incurred charges of $218M to write down and liquidate Foot Locker 
inventory. During 1Q26, DKS took additional actions to write down and liquidate $43M of additional 
unproductive inventory.
In 1Q26, FL comped modestly positive with expanding margin, with further contribution from Fast 
Break locations. The concern is that these initiatives may prove difficult to sustain, with the sales 
driven by product that was previously marked-down and costs that were written down in the prior 
period. Going forward, Foot Locker will need to generate earnings growth through more difficult levers 
such as traffic gains, market share expansion, and higher full-price sell-through. If consumer demand 
softens or competitive promotional intensity increases, especially among FL's lower-income customer 
demographic, the company could see margin momentum moderate once the benefits from inventory 
normalization and promotional discipline are fully lapped.
Nike's Business Remains Pressured. Allocation changes may be resolved, but Nike's deteriorating

(Exhibit 37). With these, we can derive the proportion of square footage growth that is due to 
relocations: FY25 square footage grew 440bps YoY, and we can calculate that true net new stores 
accounted for 320bps, and relocations accounted for 120bps driven by -360bps relocated/closed core 
DSG, +180bps FH, and +300bps HOS.
Next, we can estimate what an equivalent new store productivity is based on disclosures of these 
concepts; 1) the closed/converted DSG store is equivalent to comp productivity (set at 100% equal to 
the change in square footage); 2) FH is generating ~$14M on a 50k sq.ft box, or $280/sq ft. (~120%

total +200-275BPS impact to total comp going forward.
Further incorporating our estimate for GameChanger, a $150M business growing at ~30% YoY, 
we estimate an approximate 20-40bps impact to reported comp sales. Combining these two, 
we estimate DSG's underlying core comp of +1.0% to +1.5% for FY26E-FY28E (Exhibit 39), and 
extrapolating this implies that reported traffic may actually be negative for the underlying DSG

Threading the Needle on M&A
The overall DKS story is not straightforward, and for the DKS stock to work the company will need 
to deliver on both FL and DSG. If FL deteriorates, there is concern that the business is structurally 
flawed, and the acquisition looks like an albatross on what was otherwise an attractive business. If DSG 
deteriorates, there is additional worry that the business will not be able to support the growth and 
investments necessary to turn FL. Buying DKS requires believing that both of these banners can 
work at the same time.
The primary concerns for the combined entity are: 1) scaled M&A in US retail rarely works and may 
become a distraction for the DKS management team; 2) the international business adds complexity; 
and 3) the acquisition doubles down and increases exposure to both footwear and Nike.
Skepticism on M&A. Scaled M&A in retail has a very spotty track record, with synergies rarely 
materializing as planned, particularly when a share gainer acquires its share donor. Investors will point 
to Dollar Tree and Family Dollar as a key example. We don't think the comparison is entirely fair, as
1) the sporting goods category itself is healthier than the discount consumables category, 2) the 
market share in sporting goods is more consolidated than the consumables market, and not as easily 
substitutable in mass channels, and 3) the price paid for the business was arguably on trough level of 
FL earnings (DLTR acquired Family Dollar for nearly $9B at ~11x EV/EBITDA vs. DKS acquiring FL for 
$2.5B at ~6x EV/EBITDA).
International Business Brings Complexity. FL also brings international risk, ~30% of last reported 
sales in 2024 came from international markets. There are certainly risks that this acquisition brings 
increased complexity in running a global supply chain. However, there are also opportunities as this 
may help DKS lay the groundwork for future international expansion of DSG, while also expanding 
capabilities to better partner with its vendor base globally. UK-based JD Sports has made significant 
inroads in apparel/footwear in the US organically and through the acquisition of Hibbett, and 
strategically this allows DKS to also compete globally.
Overexposure to Footwear and Nike. While DSG has shifted towards softlines over the last decade, 
the acquisition of FL massively accelerates their exposure to footwear and Nike. We estimate that 
the combined consolidated DKS business is ~50% footwear (from ~30% at DSG alone), with Nike 
now representing ~37% of merchandise sales (from 25% at DSG alone). Risks arise from over-
concentration with Nike (clearly evidenced from what happened at FL) as the direction of the brand 
can dictate overall performance at the retailer to a greater degree. Conversely, the combination 
increases negotiating power with brands - as evidenced by the $100-$125M of procurement 
synergies that are likely to benefit DSG. All-in, we think the acquisition actually tilts the balance of 
power between retailer and brand further in DKS's favor, and Nike clearly needs DKS's help today more 
than ever in building and maintaining a healthy US marketplace, which can result in the absolute best 
allocation of product and stronger vendor allowances.

Some Structural Concerns Linger
We still have some structural concerns on the business that will be more difficult to address. First, we 
conducted multiple expert calls that describe ASO's proposition as being "stuck in the middle" - it is 
not specialized enough for enthusiasts that go to Dick's, Bass/Cabela's, REI, or Orvis, and it is not value 
enough for everyday families that can get their basics met at Walmart, Target, or Amazon. Second, 
and not entirely unrelated, the lack of access to brands was identified as the primary reason that 
ASO under-comps relative to Dick's, as the business leans more into providing value vs. elevating 
assortment and merchandising. Third, it is relatively under-developed in its omni-channel capabilities 
today, and while the company is clearly taking the right steps to address this, it will take time to grow 
the business in a non-cannibalistic way. Lastly, and in our view the thing that will take longer to fix, 
the business remains largely regional in the South and South-West, which have lower levels of income, 
category spend, and lower levels of sport and outdoor participation.
Stuck in the Middle. We conducted multiple industry expert calls, including people who worked at 
both ASO and DKS, and came away with a consistent messaging on the difficulty of ASO's positioning 
as being a dedicated value channel in sporting goods. Sporting goods enthusiasts (who drive most of 
the spend in any given category) are most likely to go to a more specialized channel (at least a DKS, 
and often even more). Value-oriented families that just need the basics to participate in sport are 
often-times able to just find suitable product at Walmart, Amazon, or Target. In our view, this makes 
for a very difficult competitive positioning, not quite able to achieve the premium offering at DKS (and 
then also not getting access to those premium brands when they are still generating high demand), 
but also not being able to fully deliver the value of the mass channel (and also not benefiting from trip 
consolidation of busy families that require all their needs met in one store).
The Value Choice Evident in Brand Performance. Our industry sources also indicated that the lack 
of access to brands is the largest reason that ASO under-comps relative to DKS. In the days pre-
IPO, the business chose to focus on the value customer and compete head-to-head with Walmart, 
leaning into private brands instead of dedicating resources to improve partnering capabilities with 
global brands. There is clear evidence of this when comparing the footwear growth of the two 
businesses, where DKS was able to accelerate its footwear sales significantly in large part by deepening 
its partnership with Nike in 2022 (Nike growth at DKS of +36%, driving Footwear growth of +16% 
(Exhibit 42)). Comparatively in the same time period, ASO's Nike business actually shrunk (-6% with 
overall Footwear sales essentially flat (Exhibit 43)). Whether it is Nike or other brands, we believe 
this dynamic remains true today, with DKS capturing the growth of softlines brands as a first-choice 
partner, and ASO getting access later in their lifecycle (Jordan decks accelerated at ASO in 2025 while 
the brand was facing slowing growth, the potential for HOKA to become available in 2026 again while 
there are questions on their own growth trajectory).

New stores have been a significant driver to the top-line as the company has shifted its strategy, and 
on top of the non-comp growth, the maturation of stores as they enter the comp base can provide

fewer options, and are often coming at a lower cost (and in some cases, driving a higher initial sales

different maturation curve, while still helping to drive a +LSD lift to comp in years 1-5. By our estimate, 
new stores that have entered the comp base are adding a ~100bps overall lift this year, which embeds 
a relatively stable comp in the legacy fleet to hit the most recent guidance of flat to +2%.
Refined Strategy Leans Into Exurb + Rural Markets. ASO laid out targets for its LRP of 450 stores 
(opening approximately 25 annually) driving an incremental $1.9B of sales (or ~$15M/store), and 
an even longer-term national expansion opportunity for 800+ stores. The company further refined 
its new store opening strategy from "inside-out" (i.e., focusing on urban locations then moving 
into suburban locations) to "outside-in" (i.e., focusing on rural locations first then working to urban 
locations), noting that stores in smaller communities provide a larger reach with higher customer 
affinity, while also costing less, and in some cases driving higher sales volume in the first year.

previously estimated, but may lead to higher productivity in our view.

Industry Primer: A Deep Dive Into the US Sporting Goods Industry
We outline the primary drivers for sporting goods demand, analyze the market and highlight key 
indicators that we believe are most relevant for investors. The underlying demand drivers for sporting 
goods remain robust, with rising trends in health and wellness across industries, further evidenced 
by higher levels of participation in sport, exercise, and recreational activities, record levels of youth 
participation of sport not only driving demand from families but feeding the funnel into adulthood, 
and continued adoption of outdoor activities. The sporting goods market is a $150B-$250B market 
(depending on definition) and has delivered long-term +LSD growth, benefiting from rising health 
and wellness participation, increased youth and recreational sports activity and consumers’ growing 
preference for active lifestyles.
It is highly fragmented, though consolidating organically and through acquisitions, with increased 
importance of softlines, branded goods, and omni-channel capabilities, all further insulating it 
against being "Amazon'ed". The sporting goods category remains relatively healthy from a 
macro perspective - with PCE growing +5% annually over the last 20 years. While the category is 
highly elastic and sensitive to price, it is currently accelerating with both units and price - driven by 
heightened levels of sports and outdoor participation, coupled with pricing in response to tariffs.

Youth Participation in Sports Is Growing
Youth participation remains the foundational driver of the U.S. sports ecosystem, acting as both a 
mirror to values held by parents prioritizing health and fitness for their children, and as a driver for 
future participants. More than half of children ages 6–17 participate in organized sports and lessons, 
generating substantial demand for equipment, leagues, travel, and related spending. Participation has 
rebounded strongly since the pandemic, rising from 49% in 2021 to 58% (Exhibit 70), highlighting 
renewed engagement across youth sports. However, access is increasingly shaped by economics, as 
participation rates range from just 42% among youth in the lowest-income bracket to 75% among 
youth from the highest-income households (Exhibit 71), while average family spending on a child’s 
primary sport has climbed 46% since 2019. Regionally, participation remains strongest in more 
affluent Northeastern and Upper Midwestern markets, while growth is increasingly being driven by 
lower-cost recreational and community-based programs that are helping expand access beyond 
traditional club sports across other regions.
The most significant divide is economic. NSCH data show participation rises sharply with income: 
only 42% of youth from families below the federal poverty threshold participate in organized sports, 
versus 75% of youth from households at 400% of poverty or above. Project Play’s 2024 Parenting 
Survey highlights why the gap persists. Average spending on a child’s primary sport reached $1,016 
in 2024, up +46% from 2019 and roughly twice the pace of overall inflation; when secondary sports 
are included, parents spend nearly $1,500 per child annually (Exhibit 72). High income households 
spend 1.5x the total average on equipment and uniforms, and over 2.5x the low income household.
Interestingly, the divergence is so high that high income households spend 1.3x on secondary sports 
for their children what low income households spend on primary sports. Further, Aspen Institute 
researchers estimate total family spending on youth sports now exceeds $40 billion per year, creating 
a growing pay-to-play barrier that disproportionately affects lower-income households.
Regionally, participation remains highest in many affluent Northeastern and Upper Midwestern states 
and lowest across portions of the South, with NSCH state-level data showing a wide range—from 
roughly ~50% in the least-active states to more than 70% in the highest-participation states (Exhibit 
73). Recent growth appears strongest in markets benefiting from expanded recreational, school-
based, and community offerings rather than expensive club models, primarily in the Southwest (Exhibit 
74). A noteworthy trend identified by Project Play is that participation has rebounded nationally even 
as costs have surged, suggesting growth is increasingly concentrated in lower-cost and entry-level 
formats.

Outdoor Participation Trends
Participation in Outdoor activities is at an all-time high, with growth driven by high income cohorts 
and an aging population. Overall participation for those aged over 6 reached over 60% in 2024, up 
from 50% in 2007, with over 180M participants in the US, and growing most recently at +3% (Exhibit 
75). By income group, the growth is most notable with higher income cohorts (growing +5% over the 
2020-2024 time period) (Exhibit 76). Additionally, penetration rates per person expanded the highest 
in the West South Central region (+840bps from 2020 to 2024), the Middle Atlantic (+680bps), 
and Mountain (+670bps) (Exhibit 77). Further, the oldest age cohort is the fastest growing group 
within the population, which is driving the average age of outdoor participation higher, and more 
than offsetting the second-highest group (the youngest population); i.e. people are starting outdoor 
activities earlier, and are continuing them longer in life (Exhibit 78). Lastly, outdoor participation is 
rarely mono-category, and the highest activity (hiking) is accompanied by additional activities 85% of 
the time: i.e. it is easy to get into and often a gateway to other activities (Exhibit 79).
Outdoor recreation participation remains one of the strongest growth segments in the broader 
active-lifestyle market. According to the Outdoor Industry Association’s 2025 Outdoor Participation 
Trends Report, a record 181M people in the US (over 60% of the population age six and older) 
participated in outdoor recreation in 2024, up +3% YoY. Participation growth was especially broad-
based, with hiking, camping, fishing, bicycling, and running each adding more than two million 
participants, while the number of “core” participants (those engaging most frequently) increased 
by roughly five million, reversing a decade-long stagnation. The data suggest outdoor recreation 
continues to benefit from long-term shifts toward health and wellness, nature-based experiences, 
flexible recreation formats, and demand for lower-cost leisure activities relative to other forms of 
entertainment.
The demographic profile of participation is also becoming broader and more diverse. OIA reports 
particularly strong growth among seniors (+7.4%) and youth (+5.6%), pointing to a more multi-
generational participant base, while Black (+12.8%) and Hispanic (+11.8%) participation grew faster 
than the overall market. From an income perspective, higher-income households (>$100,000 annually) 
were a notable driver of growth, reflecting their greater ability to spend on gear, travel, and premium 
experiences, though participation gains extended across demographic groups. Regionally, growth 
has been strongest in the South, which added participants at a faster pace than other regions and 
continues to benefit from population migration trends, expanding outdoor infrastructure, and 
favorable climate conditions. Importantly, the biggest gains are occurring in accessible “gateway” 
activities such as hiking, camping, fishing, and walking—activities with relatively low barriers to entry 
that often serve as feeders into more specialized outdoor pursuits, supporting a durable pipeline for 
future participation and outdoor-related consumer spending.

The Market
The sporting goods market is a $150B-$250B market (depending on definition) and has delivered 
long-term +LSD growth, benefiting from rising health and wellness participation, increased youth and 
recreational sports activity, and consumers’ growing preference for active lifestyles. 1) Over the past 
several decades, the industry has become increasingly consolidated, with a handful of large national 
retailers and vertically integrated brands gaining share through scale, merchandising expertise, and 
omni-channel investments. 2) At the same time, the category has shifted meaningfully toward 
softlines—particularly athletic apparel, footwear, and lifestyle products—which have expanded 
the addressable market beyond traditional equipment sales and driven higher-margin growth. 3)

Divergent Brand Stories - National Brands vs. Owned
For both DKS and ASO, Nike represents the largest supplier. For DKS, Nike represents approximately

E-Commerce Is a Key Point of Differentiation
In the highly fragmented sporting goods market, omni-channel capabilities have become a significant 
point of differentiation and were rapidly accelerated during the COVID-19 period of lockdowns. Today, 
approximately 16% of DKS's consolidated business (DSG +FL) is done through e-commerce, from just 
12% from standalone DSG in 2017 (Exhibit 87), though the lines are blurred with stores serving as 
omni-channel fulfillment (90% of sales enabled by stores, and over 80% of online orders fulfilled by 
stores). The ability to shop wherever, however, and whenever across the country sets DKS apart from 
competition given their national presence, and the capabilities are un-matchable from smaller mono-
store competitors. Our industry work suggests that in the years leading up to COVID-19, DKS made 
the investments in their own capabilities, including data and analytics, supply chain and merchandise 
allocation, to be able to rapidly set up capabilities in-house such as buy online pickup in store (BOPIS) 
and ship from store. With scale and the ownership of the process, e-commerce became a profit-
neutral transaction for the business - it was no longer cannibalizing and deleveraging to the fixed cost 
structure.
Comparatively, ASO's e-commerce business today is approximately 12%, up from 4% in FY17, 
and while the company expects to grow to 15% of the business over the next five years, it is much 
earlier in its current efforts. The company is currently making additional investments to improve the 
capabilities, making the website easier to navigate, and building in AI agentic search and incorporating 
a stronger loyalty program, which should all be future drivers of adoption.

Store Location Analysis
DKS has the largest national retail footprint within sporting goods with 888 locations as of the end of 
2025 (including specialty banners), with stores in every state except Montana, Alaska, and Hawaii. ASO 
is a largely regional sporting goods retailer, with 322 stores predominantly in the South/Southwest

the other's core market (i.e. since 2019, DKS opened 8 locations in Texas and 5 in Florida, the most of 
any state, and ASO is moving north, opening 8 in Indiana, 5 in North Carolina, 3 in Ohio and Virginia 
each, and 2 in Pennsylvania).
DKS employs a market-dominant location strategy, concentrating large-format stores in densely 
populated suburban trade areas with strong demographics, often near major power centers and 
complementary retailers, while leveraging its broad geographic footprint to support omni-channel 
fulfillment and local market share gains. ASO follows a lower-cost expansion strategy focused primarily 
on suburban and secondary markets across the South and Midwest, targeting family-oriented, value-
conscious consumers with stores located in convenient, high-traffic retail corridors that can generate 
attractive returns with a more modest capital investment.

The Key Macro Indicators
The sporting goods category remains relatively healthy from a macro perspective - with PCE growing 
+5% annually over the last 20 years. While the category is highly elastic and sensitive to price, it is 
currently accelerating with both units and price, driven by heightened levels of sports and outdoor 
participation, coupled with pricing in response to tariffs. The amount consumers spend on the 
category has remained elevated in the years following COVID lockdowns in 2020/2021 (now 2% of 
annual spending, up 40bps from historical levels), and this benefit appears durable. Lastly, retail sales 
for the category coincide with the data we see in PCE - sporting goods has accelerated over the last 18 
months and the most recent data suggests it is outpacing overall retail spending.
Sporting Goods PCE
Over the last 20 years, the Personal Consumption Expenditure (PCE) for the Sporting Goods category 
has grown at an annual +5% (+4% excluding the 2020/2021 COVID-19 bump). Like apparel and 
footwear, the category itself is generally deflationary except for a few instances (2021/2022, 2026 
with tariff pricing) (Exhibit 94). This year, consumption in the category has accelerated through June, 
with an acceleration in both units and price (Exhibit 95). The category does include ammo, which was 
likely a driver of April acceleration (ammo as a category accelerates in periods of conflict/war).
Notably, as a percent of overall PCE, Sporting Goods now represents ~2.0% of overall personal 
consumption expenditures on goods, from a historic rate closer to 1.6% (Exhibit 96). While down from 
COVID highs of 2.2%, spending in the category has rebased higher from pre-COVID levels, and in our 
view is durable with both higher sport and outdoor participation trends.

Sporting Goods Elasticity
Utilizing PCE changes in quantity and changes in price, we can estimate the elasticity of reported 
categories (i.e. how does volume react relative to changes in price). Since 2000 (and excluding the 
2020/2021 COVID-impacted periods), price declined in 83% of the months, as the category is 
generally deflationary. Using this time frame, the elasticity of Sporting Goods, Guns, and Ammunition 
is -1.35 (i.e. every 1% change in price results in a 1.35% change in volume) (Exhibit 97), which is 
on par with children's clothing, which has notoriously little pricing power (Exhibit 98). To us, this 
intuitively makes sense, as the sporting goods, guns, and ammunition spending domestically is a 
largely discretionary spending decision, and cost is a significant barrier to outdoor participation 
(evidenced by the participation rates referenced earlier by income cohort).

Investment Thesis, Valuation and Risks
Academy Sports and Outdoors, Inc. (ASO)
Investment Thesis
We are Equal Weight ASO. While there are signs of underlying improvements, the company still must show sustainable positive comps 
to drive leverage over the SG&A investments made over the last 3 years. Further, if comps turn negative, we believe the square footage 
growth story may be at risk. On the positive side, ASO continues to generate robust FCF and aggressively repurchase shares, which should 
buoy EPS growth, and new stores entering comp base are seeing strength (FY22-FY24 vintage +MSD).
Target Price Valuation for ASO
Our $50 price target is based on 7.5x P/E our 2027E GAAP EPS.
We believe the multiple below the peer group is warranted given the history of negative comps, weaker 2QTD trends and a bearish outlook 
on the low-end macro.
Risks to Our Price Target and Rating for ASO
Upside risks include: (1) comps improve faster than anticipated; (2) GM (specifically merch margins) improve at a faster rate than 
anticipated, (3) existing initiatives for omni-channel cost less or retail media benefit materializes
Downside risks include (1) core lower- and middle-income customer faces inflationary pressure, (2) failure for comps to inflect positive 
putting square footage growth story at risk, (3) margin erosion from higher investment in price and promo to drive traffic
 
Dick's Sporting Goods, Inc. (DKS)
Investment Thesis
We are Overweight rated on DKS. We believe 1) there is compelling upside for a FL recovery taking the DKS playbook on merchandising 
and vendor relationships and modest recovery in margin embedded today; 2) core Dick's Sporting Goods is the established leader 
operating at structurally higher productivity levels; and 3) initiatives in new stores, GameChanger, paid loyalty, and Retail Media are in the 
early innings of a differentiated flywheel that can drive profitable growth with key brands.
Target Price Valuation for DKS
Our price target of $240 applies ~14.5x our FY27 EPS estimate of $16.50, above the historical average ~13x given continued momentum 
in the model, driven by new store formats and upside from the Foot Locker acquisition - which is in the early part of its recovery.
Risks to Our Price Target and Rating for DKS
Downside risks (1) comps decelerate and/or turn negative; (2) EBIT margin gains reverse; (3) Acquisition of FL proves to be long-term 
dilutive or takes longer to turn.
 
NIKE, Inc. (NKE)
Investment Thesis
We rate NKE Equal Weight. 1) While the North America business is improving as the headwind from classics dissipates, the international 
market is deteriorating and is likely to drag on results near term, 2) There are limited catalysts between now and "Win Now" actions ending 
by year end and "Sports Offense" impacting Spring 2027; and 3) We see little upside to out-year earnings and very little valuation support 
for what is proving to be a muted and prolonged recovery.
Target Price Valuation for NKE
Our $40 price target is based on a ~22.5x P/E on our CY27E EPS of $1.75. This is in line with the current consensus 22x NTM P/E and in 
line with our view of a longer road for a more muted sales and margin recovery.
Risks to Our Price Target and Rating for NKE
Downside risks: (1) new innovation (along w/ franchise reset) does not drive topline; (2) global demand remains tepid; (3) the transition 
back to wholesale from DTC causes more material headwinds to profits in the NT; and 4) International headwinds accelerate and 
necessitate a larger restructuring of the business. Upside risks include (1) a positive turn in Chinese consumption trends and (2) better-
than-expected performance in US clearance activity on both sales and margins.
