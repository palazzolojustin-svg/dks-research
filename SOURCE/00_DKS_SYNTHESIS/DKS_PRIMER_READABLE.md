# Dick's Sporting Goods: A Readable Primer

*October 2026. Stock at $137.34. Written for the Point72 Academy pitch.*

A quick housekeeping note before we start. Dick's fiscal year ends around the end of January, so "FY2025" means the year that ended in January 2026, and "FY2026" is the year we're in now, ending January 2027. Bloomberg labels everything one year higher, which will confuse you exactly once and then never again.

---

## The short version

Dick's Sporting Goods is the best-run big-box sporting goods retailer in America, and for about four years it was one of the best retail stocks you could own. Then, in May 2025, it agreed to buy Foot Locker. In August 2026 it told investors that Foot Locker was going to lose money this year instead of making $100 to $150 million, and that the core Dick's business was going to be a bit less profitable too. Earnings guidance dropped from $13.50–$14.50 a share to $11.00–$12.00. The stock fell 31% in a day, after already falling 25% in the two months before.

So now you have a stock at roughly 10 times next year's earnings, which is cheap for a company with Dick's track record, attached to a Foot Locker business that the market seems to think is worth less than nothing. The question is whether that's right.

My answer: I'm long, but carefully. I think the core Dick's business is a little worse than the Street believes, and I think Foot Locker is a lot less bad than the stock price implies. The second effect is bigger, and it has a nearer catalyst. My base case is about $154 a share in 12 to 24 months, plus a dividend yield of 3.6%, with a bull case around $209 and a bear case around $93. Weight those 50/25/25 and you get about $152, or roughly a 14–15% total return with upside about 1.6 times the downside.

That's not a screaming trade. It's a decent one with a specific reason to exist. The rest of this document explains why.

## 1. How Dick's makes money

Dick's runs about 890 large stores in the US. The classic Dick's store is a 50,000-square-foot "store of stores": team sports, apparel, footwear, golf, fitness, outdoor. On top of that it now runs two newer formats. **House of Sport** is the flagship: 100,000-plus square feet with a climbing wall, turf field, batting cages, golf simulators and a premium sneaker deck. **Field House** is a smaller version of the same idea and is, in management's words, "the workhorse." There are about 41 Houses of Sport and 52 Field Houses today, plus Golf Galaxy, an outlet chain called Going Going Gone!, and a youth-sports app called GameChanger.

The economic engine is simple. Dick's gets access to the good stuff from the big brands, especially Nike, and sells it at full price. Nike alone is about a quarter of what the core business buys. Back in 2017 Dick's reorganized its vendors into tiers and climbed back to the top of Nike's wholesale list, and that brand access is the single biggest reason it has consistently out-comped rivals like Academy. On top of the national brands, Dick's sells its own "vertical" brands (DSG, CALIA, VRST and others) at about 13% of sales, and those carry margins 700 to 900 basis points higher than the brands it resells.

Profit comes from three places. First, merchandise margin: selling allocated, in-demand product at full price, plus mix toward the higher-margin house brands. Second, leverage on a mostly fixed cost base of rent and labor. When sales grow faster than those costs, margins expand. Third, a few small but very profitable side businesses: GameChanger did nearly $150 million of revenue last year at very high margins, and a retail-media business that sells ads to brands has just turned profitable.

Here's what the core business looked like last year: $14.1 billion of sales and $1.57 billion of segment profit, an 11.1% margin. Merchandise cost ate about half of sales, store labor about 14%, rent and occupancy about 8.5%, and everything else about 16%. Compare that to 2019, when Dick's earned a 5% operating margin. The company more than doubled its margin in six years, roughly half from better merchandise margins and half from spreading rent over more sales.

Two things about this machine matter for the investment case.

The first is that most of Dick's new stores aren't really new. About three-quarters of House of Sport and Field House "openings" are relocations or conversions of existing Dick's stores. Under Dick's accounting, a relocated store stays in the comparable-sales base. So if an old 50,000-square-foot box becomes a House of Sport doing twice the volume, that shows up as comp growth. Wells Fargo estimates relocations add 2 to 2.75 percentage points to the comp every year, which would leave the "underlying" comp at only about 1 to 1.5%. Dick's has also changed its comp definition three times since 2022 (adding relocations, then GameChanger, then its warehouse-sale stores). None of this is shady, but it means the headline comp flatters the organic health of the business.

The second is that Dick's is spending a lot on that store build-out. Capex went from about $364 million in FY2022 to $1.14 billion last year and is guided to about $1.4 billion net this year. That's more than twice depreciation, which means depreciation expense is going to keep climbing for years and eat into margins. Management's own numbers say a House of Sport costs a bit over $20 million, does about $35 million of first-year sales and earns about a 25% cash-on-cash return. Those are good numbers if you believe them. Note, though, that the sales figure doesn't net out cannibalization of the old store, and that the target of "75 to 100 Houses of Sport by 2027" quietly disappeared from filings this year. The actual plan now gets to about 67.

Free cash flow has been shrinking as a result: roughly $940 million in FY2023, $509 million in FY2024, $400 million last year, and only $49 million in the first half of this year. The dividend alone costs about $450 million a year.

## 2. The industry

Sporting goods in the US is a $150 to $250 billion market, depending on how you define it, growing low single digits. Dick's has about 9% of its own definition of the market and around 20% of the brick-and-mortar sporting goods and athletic footwear channel.

Here's the most interesting fact in the industry data: over the last decade, Dick's gained about 4.5 points of market share, and Foot Locker lost about 3.7 points. Put them together and the combined company is roughly where the two were in 2016. Dick's didn't buy a growth business. It bought the share donor.

Demand is healthy in a structural sense. Youth sports participation is at record highs, families spend about $1,000 a year on a kid's primary sport, and gym membership and outdoor participation are both at records. Amazon has been stuck at about 13% of the category for years because people want to try on shoes and don't want to ship a basketball hoop.

The catch is footwear, and footwear means Nike. From roughly 2017 to 2023 Nike pulled its hottest product away from wholesale partners and pushed it through its own stores and website. Foot Locker got hit hardest: its share of Nike's "high heat" allocation roughly halved. Then Nike changed CEOs and reversed course, shipping huge volumes back into wholesale. North American wholesale grew 14% last fiscal year, with one quarter up 24%. One analyst called it the largest wholesale sell-in in Nike's history.

That flood of product is now the problem. Lifestyle sneakers like the Air Force 1, Dunk and Jordan 1 are piling up, more than 30% of Nike's assortment is on discount at retail partners, and Nike itself is guiding revenue down high single digits this year. Running shoes and "brown shoes" like UGG and Birkenstock are strong. Retro lifestyle product is not. Dick's is relatively insulated because it skews toward performance product. Foot Locker, as Ed Stack put it, is "more dependent on launch and retro product."

Among competitors, Academy is the most direct comparison and it's struggling: its comps have been negative most years since 2022, and its traffic fell more than 5% last quarter while Dick's comped nearly 5%. JD Sports, which owns Finish Line and Hibbett, is seeing North American sales decline mid-single digits. The brands themselves (On, HOKA, Nike) are all trying to grow their own stores and websites while still needing Dick's for reach.

## 3. Macro: what actually matters

Most macro factors matter only a little. Four matter a lot.

**The Nike inventory cycle** is the big one, for the reasons above. It peaks right now, in the fall of 2026, and should be cleaner by spring 2027. Everyone agrees on the direction. The debate is how long it lasts.

**Tariffs and tariff refunds.** After the Supreme Court ruling on emergency tariffs, Dick's got about $59 million back in the second quarter. Most of it was excluded from adjusted earnings as a one-time item, but about $21 million stayed in, which flattered core gross margin by roughly half a point. That's nice this year and a headwind next year, when the company has to lap it.

**The lower- and middle-income consumer.** Gas prices are up on Middle East conflict, and interest-rate expectations have flipped from cuts to possible hikes. Academy, which skews lower-income, is feeling it in traffic. Dick's used to say on every call that it saw "growth across all income demographics" with no trading down. In August, that line disappeared.

**Event comparisons.** The 2026 World Cup juiced the first half of this year, and the Knicks' playoff run helped too. Next year Dick's has to lap both.

What doesn't matter much: housing, cotton and freight costs (which are actually lower), and Amazon.

## 4. Management, governance and capital allocation

The Stack family has run this company since Dick Stack opened a bait-and-tackle shop in Binghamton in 1948. Ed Stack, his son, took over with two stores in 1984 and built it into what it is. He became Executive Chairman in 2021, when Lauren Hobart became CEO, and he personally runs the Foot Locker integration. The family's Class B shares carry 10 votes each, which gives them about 78% of the vote. Nobody is forcing anything on this company.

Management's track record splits cleanly in two. On the core business, they under-promise and over-deliver like clockwork. FY2024 guidance was raised three times and still beaten. FY2025 guidance was raised twice and beaten again. On Foot Locker, they have been wrong twice in nine months. They said the deal would be accretive to earnings in the first full year; it's now about 20% dilutive. They raised Foot Locker guidance in May and slashed it to a loss in August. The Street has learned to trust the core guidance and discount the Foot Locker guidance, and that's probably correct.

There are some governance yellow flags. In January 2026 the company bought a $49 million aircraft from an LLC that Ed Stack owns, approved by the audit committee on an independent valuation. In June 2025 shareholders approved a fivefold increase in authorized Class B shares, which drew the most opposition of anything on the ballot. Votes withheld against Stack rose from about 1.7 million to 10.8 million this year. None of this is disqualifying, but it's the kind of thing that keeps a multiple from expanding.

**Insider trading** tells an interesting story. Before the crash, CEO Lauren Hobart sold about $4.6 million of stock at around $228, the day after the first-quarter results. After the crash, seven insiders bought a combined $5.6 million at an average of about $131. That includes the CFO, Navdeep Gupta, who bought about $1 million, and Matthew Barnes, who runs Foot Locker International and bought about $500,000. Hobart and Stack did not buy.

**Capital allocation** has shifted from buybacks to stores. Dick's bought back $649 million of stock in FY2023, then $268 million, $342 million, and $141 million in the first quarter of this year, all at prices between about $196 and $219. It bought nothing in the second quarter, when the stock was falling. The dividend went up 105% in 2023 and 10% in 2024 and 2025, then only 3% this year to $1.25 a quarter.

**The "2026 debt."** There is no debt maturing in 2026. What people mean is the $1 billion of bonds Dick's sold in late September 2026: $400 million at 6.2% due 2036 and $600 million at 6.9% due 2056, rated Baa2/BBB. That roughly doubles the company's annual interest bill, from about $70 million to about $137 million. The prospectus lists "repurchases of shares" among the uses of proceeds. It priced one month after the stock crashed, on the same day the CFO bought $1 million of stock.

You can read that three ways. It's a war chest for a big buyback. It's insurance in case Foot Locker keeps eating cash. Or it's money for Foot Locker restructuring costs. I lean toward the first. A billion dollars at today's price would retire about 8% of the shares. The bond market reads it more nervously: Dick's bonds trade wide of peers, and Bloomberg's credit analysts have flagged that rating pressure "could emerge." Management hasn't said which it is. The November earnings call is the first chance.

## 5. The Foot Locker deal

### Why Dick's bought it

The official logic: Foot Locker gives Dick's a younger, more urban, more diverse sneaker customer it doesn't reach. It gives an all-US company an international business. Dick's thinks it can bring "Retail 101" discipline and its brand relationships to a chain that lost its way, and it promised $100 to $125 million of cost savings. Ed Stack called it a decision "for a lifetime."

The unofficial logic, which you can piece together from the filings: Nike was swinging back toward wholesale, Foot Locker is still its largest sneaker-specialty partner, and owning both gives Dick's enormous scale with Nike. Foot Locker was also cheap, at about 0.3 times sales.

### The price path tells the whole story

This is the most revealing part of the merger documents. Stack first approached Foot Locker CEO Mary Dillon in January 2024. Foot Locker said no. In June 2024 Dick's offered $31 to $34 a share, then went to $37, then $40 as "best and final." Foot Locker wanted $41. Then, in November 2024, Dick's walked away, saying its diligence showed Foot Locker "required a much more significant turnaround effort than it had anticipated" and that it couldn't validate management's financial projections.

Four months later Dick's came back at $24, having quietly bought a 4.5% stake in the open market and threatening to take its offer public. The parties agreed on $25.50 in April. Then Foot Locker's first quarter came in worse than expected, Dick's said it was "very concerned" the business was declining faster than it thought, and the final price was $24 in cash or 0.1168 Dick's shares. Total consideration was about $2.5 billion. About 86% of Foot Locker holders chose stock.

In other words, Dick's did its homework, concluded the business was in worse shape than management claimed, walked away, came back and bought it anyway at 40% less. That's a buyer who knew what it was getting. It's also a buyer who has now had to admit the turnaround is taking longer than even its own skeptical diligence assumed.

### What Foot Locker was

Foot Locker's operating margin was 9% to 12% as recently as FY2018 to FY2021. Then it fell apart: about 8% in 2022, then about 2.5% in 2023 and 2024, then negative. Wells Fargo attributes more than half of that decline to Nike cutting its allocation. Nike went from 75% of Foot Locker's purchases in 2020 to about 59% in 2024. Dillon's 2023 "Lace Up" plan promised an 8.5–9% margin by 2026. Foot Locker never got close.

### What it's been like under Dick's

Not great. Foot Locker comps since the deal closed have run −4.7%, −3.4%, +0.6% and −3.6%. Segment profit went −$46 million, −$6 million, +$17 million and −$32 million. Dick's has booked $516 million of deal-related charges so far, including a $218 million inventory write-down in the first quarter it owned the business ("clean out the garage"), and expects up to $750 million in total. It has closed 67 underperforming stores, and the WSS chain has gone from 151 stores to 99.

The main fix is a store refresh program called "Fast Break," now in more than 250 stores and heading to 300–350 by year-end. Dick's also hired two new presidents: Ann Freeman, a longtime Nike North America executive, for the US, and Matthew Barnes, formerly CEO of Aldi Süd and Tesco UK, for International.

The most important thing management has said recently came on the August call. An analyst asked whether Dick's would consider rightsizing or monetizing parts of Foot Locker, and Stack answered: "simply yes." A few weeks later, at a conference, management described "creating a menu" of options with "no preconceived ideas."

### What it's worth

This is the crux of the bull case, so it's worth doing the math. Strip out Foot Locker and the core Dick's business should earn something like $12.60 a share next year. At 12 times that, the core alone is worth about $151 a share, or roughly $13.5 billion. The whole company currently trades at about $12.4 billion. The difference, about −$1.1 billion, is what the market is implicitly assigning to Foot Locker.

A negative value is only rational if Foot Locker burns cash forever. But the losses are concentrated in pieces Dick's can cut. International has 681 stores, mostly in Europe, with comps around −8%. Its losses also get no tax benefit, which pushes the whole company's tax rate up about four points, worth roughly 65 cents a share a year on its own. Foot Locker capex is running about $400 million a year and could be cut in half. Champs has 364 stores, many within a mile of a Foot Locker. And Foot Locker's leases are short, 5 to 10 years, so shrinking it is cheaper and faster than it looks.

Just getting Foot Locker from −$1.1 billion to zero is worth about $12 a share. That's the option.

## 6. The numbers

### The long view

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue ($B) | 12.4 | 13.0 | 13.4 | 17.2 | 22.1 | 22.6 |
| Core comp | −0.5% | +2.6% | +5.2% | +4.5% | ~3.4% | ~2.6% |

| Free cash flow ($M) | 558 | 940 | 509 | 400 | ~500 | ~458 |

The FY2026 and FY2027 figures are Bloomberg consensus.

The short version: core earnings grew about 6% a year from FY2023 to FY2025. Foot Locker has since dragged consolidated earnings back below where they were in FY2022, on almost 80% more revenue.

### The quarter-by-quarter story since the deal closed

| Quarter | Core comp | Core margin | vs. last year | Foot Locker comp | Foot Locker profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q3 FY25 | +5.7% | 8.9% | −55bp | −4.7% | −$46M (partial quarter) |
| Q4 FY25 | +3.1% | 11.0% | +88bp | −3.4% | −$6M |
| Q1 FY26 | +6.0% | 10.7% | −66bp | +0.6% | +$17M |
| Q2 FY26 | +4.9% | 12.6% | −42bp | −3.6% | −$32M |

Look at the core margin column. Comps were healthy, between 3% and 6%, yet margin fell in three of the four quarters. Strip out the tariff refund and the most recent quarter was down nearly a full point. That's the heart of my first variant view, which I'll come back to.

### The August cut, dissected

At the midpoint, guidance went from $14.00 to $11.50 a share, down $2.50. Operating income came down $260 million: about $190 million from Foot Locker and $70 million from the core. The tax rate going from 27% to 29% accounts for about 35 cents of the cut on its own. Core comp guidance was held at 2.5–4.0%, but core margin guidance came down from 11.0–11.4% to 10.6–10.9%. So the core's problem is margin, not sales.

### What has to happen for the rest of this year

Take the full-year guidance and subtract the first half, and the second half implies core profit roughly flat to down 5%, a Foot Locker loss of $26 to $66 million, and EPS of $4.57 to $5.57. Consensus sits at $5.01, a little below the midpoint. It's already pricing in a weak third quarter: core margin down about 1.75 points year over year, Foot Locker losing about $71 million, and EPS of $1.39, down a third from last year. Management has said the third quarter will be harder than the fourth, with margin pressure "most pronounced in Q3."

### What has to happen for next year

Consensus FY2027 EPS of $13.36 is up 16% on revenue growth of only about 2%. That's a margin-recovery bet: core profit up about 6%, Foot Locker swinging from a $73 million loss to a $20 million profit, and a lower tax rate. Standing in the way: lapping the tariff refunds, the World Cup and the Knicks, an extra $66 million of annual interest from the new bonds that consensus has barely modeled, and depreciation that keeps climbing as the store build-out continues.

## 7. What the Street thinks

The Street is mostly still bullish, but bruised. There are 11 Buys, 5 Holds and one Underperform (BMO, with a $110 target). The average target is about $158. The four brokers who've done the most work (Wells Fargo, JPMorgan, UBS and Barclays) cut their average target from $260 to $175, and their FY2027 estimates from $16.68 to $13.43.

The consensus story has four parts:

1. **The core is fine and Foot Locker is the problem.** Barclays says the core is in "hunt" mode and Foot Locker is in a "trap."
2. **Footwear is going through a Nike-driven inventory correction that clears by 2027.** JPMorgan's analogy is 2017, when Dick's merchandise margin fell about 2 points in the fall, then recovered. Its line: "short the inventory build, own the clean up."
3. **Foot Locker's margin repair is delayed a year, not dead.** Wells Fargo's bull case still has Foot Locker reaching a 7–8% margin by FY2028.
4. **Valuation is near trough levels.**

The bears argue Foot Locker is structurally broken, Nike is broadening distribution to lower-end retailers, and the footwear slump has further to run.

What the Street seems to be missing:

- **New debt.** None of the major equity models included it. In fact, Wells Fargo's bull case assumed debt would go down.
- **A cause for the core margin decline beyond promotions.** Almost nobody is asking whether the core margin decline is structural rather than promotional.
- **Comp quality.** Only Wells Fargo has seriously dug into how much relocations flatter the comp.

**Positioning:** short interest is 13.7% of the float and rising. The stock trades at 11.9 times this year's earnings and 10.3 times next year's, against 17–18 times at its recent peaks. Options imply about a 10% move on the next earnings report.

## 8. What the primary research says

### The expert calls

There are 14 expert interviews in the library, all from June and July 2026, with people at brands that sell to Dick's: Nike, Under Armour, Jordan, Crocs, UGG, Carhartt and others. The best sources were also the most cautious. A Nike planner said growth was 0–5%, Under Armour said mid-to-high single digits, and Jordan was "conservative." The flashy triple-digit growth numbers came from people opening new accounts or with obvious axes to grind.

The single most useful quote came from Under Armour in July, six weeks before the crash: "DICK'S and the marketplace at large are definitely getting more promotional... That's more of a gross margin situation on their side." That was exactly right, and it was the only call that flagged the margin problem. Several vendors also described Dick's turning cautious on inventory: asking for markdown protection, buying more at the last minute, committing only a month out.

The vendors that sell to Foot Locker were positive about it. They were wrong.

For the November report, the expert work suggests three things. Vendors aren't eating markdowns, so promotional costs land on Dick's own margins. Inventory could be growing at or above sales at the end of the third quarter. And nobody knows what Foot Locker's third quarter looks like.

### The card and foot-traffic data

The alternative data needs careful handling, because it contains a trap. From the time Foot Locker was acquired, Dick's credit card spending showed year-over-year growth of 15% to 58% every single week. That's because Foot Locker's sales were suddenly in the data. Once the acquisition anniversary passed in September, the growth vanished overnight: spending was down about 3% in each of the next three weeks. That compares with the sporting goods category down about 8% and discretionary retail down about 3%.

Adjusting for how the data panel has historically under-tracked Dick's, that probably translates to reported revenue growth somewhere between flat and +5% for those weeks. Consensus has third-quarter revenue roughly flat against a pro forma base, so the card data is in line with that, maybe slightly ahead. Bloomberg's headline estimate of 46% growth is a leftover from the pre-anniversary weeks. Don't read it as a beat.

In August, before the anniversary, the sporting goods category was down 6–17% depending on the dataset, while Dick's was well ahead. That supports the idea that Dick's is still taking share.

### Putting it together

The August report was a margin event, not a sales event. Core comps were in line, but Foot Locker missed and margins disappointed everywhere. For November, revenue probably isn't the problem. Gross margin is the swing factor, and consensus already has core margin down about 1.75 points. The inventory number at quarter-end will tell you a lot about the fourth quarter and next year.

## 9. The debates, ranked by how much they move the stock

A few rules of thumb for next year's earnings:

| Change | Earnings per share |
|---|---|
| Every $50 million of Foot Locker profit | ±41 cents |
| Every half-point of core margin | ±63 cents |
| Every point of core comp | ±31 cents |
| A $1 billion buyback at today's price | +75 cents |

**1. What happens to Foot Locker?** This is the biggest one, worth $15 to $25 a share either way. The bulls say Foot Locker's collapse was mostly about losing Nike allocation, Nike needs Foot Locker again, and the worst pieces can be cut off. The bears say it's a declining mall sneaker chain whose biggest supplier is building its own stores and expanding into discount channels. The evidence so far: comps negative in three of four quarters, guidance slashed, half a billion in charges, and a 9% smaller store base. No broker has modeled what happens if Dick's simply exits or licenses Europe. What would settle it: whether the November call brings a concrete plan, and whether Foot Locker comps turn positive in the fourth quarter, when it laps last year's clearance.

**2. Is the core margin decline temporary or structural?** Worth $7 to $12 a share. The bull view is that this is promotions, and promotions end. The bear view, which is mine, is that the core margin fell even when comps were strong, SG&A leveraged in only one of the last seven quarters, depreciation is climbing on twice-depreciation capex, and half the comp is bought with relocation spending. What would settle it: whether the core levers expenses in the second half on a 1–2% comp, and what management guides for core margin in March.

**3. How long does the footwear correction last?** This one is about timing. The bull view says it peaks this fall, consistent with Nike's own guidance, and spring 2027 order books look healthier. The bear view says Nike itself says its lifestyle reset runs past this fiscal year, and there's no replacement for the old hit franchises. Watch Nike's investor day on November 16–17 and its December earnings.

**4. What's the billion dollars for?** Worth about 75 cents a share of earnings and a lot of sentiment. Bulls see a buyback coming at 10 times earnings. Bears see a company borrowing at nearly 7% to cover a dividend that free cash flow no longer covers. The November call should tell us.

**5. Are House of Sport returns as good as advertised?** This is a multiple question: is this a 10x stock or a 14x stock? Management says 25% cash-on-cash returns. The skeptics point to relocations, cannibalization, three comp-definition changes and a store target that quietly shrank.

**6. Can you trust management?** Another multiple question. The core track record is excellent. The Foot Locker track record, the aircraft and the insider selling before the crash are not.

## 10. My take

### The call

**Long, half position now.** I'd add if Dick's announces concrete Foot Locker actions or a big buyback. I'd avoid sizing up before the March 2027 guidance.

| Scenario | Probability | FY2027 EPS | Multiple | Value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bull | 25% | $14.93 | 14x | ~$209 |
| Base | 50% | $12.67 | 12x | ~$154 |
| Bear | 25% | $10.42 | 9x | ~$93 |

Probability-weighted, that's about $152, roughly +11% on price, or +14–15% including the dividend.

The base case assumes core margin of about 10.5% (below consensus), Foot Locker roughly breakeven, a full year of the new interest expense, and about $700 million of buybacks. Even with a below-consensus core and Foot Locker valued at zero, you get about $150. You're getting the Foot Locker option for free, and maybe being paid to take it.

### The near term

For the November 24 report, I expect third-quarter EPS around $1.20, below the $1.39 consensus. I'd put about 40% odds on a beat. I'd put 65% odds on the core comp beating the 1.7% consensus, because the card data looks fine. Most likely, guidance is held or narrowed toward the low end (75%), with a 20% chance of a cut below $11. There's about a 40% chance management announces something concrete on Foot Locker.

Is the stock up or down on the day? Honestly, a coin flip. Expectations have been cut in half, short interest is high and positioning is washed out, which helps. A likely EPS miss hurts.

The bigger near-term risk is the March 2027 guidance for next year. Dick's has a habit of guiding conservatively in March, and I'd put 65% odds that next year's guidance comes in below consensus. A credible Foot Locker restructuring plan would neutralize that. I'd put about 55% odds on a buyback of $500 million or more by then.

### Variant view #1: the core isn't the safe half

Everyone treats core Dick's as the reliable part of the story and Foot Locker as the problem. I think the core is quietly de-levering. The margin fell in three of four quarters despite good comps. Expenses almost never grow slower than sales anymore. Depreciation is going to keep rising. And about half the comp is coming from relocated stores, not from more customers buying more stuff in the same box. I think next year's core profit comes in around $1.60 billion, not the $1.65 billion the Street has, which puts EPS closer to $12.50 than $13.36 before any buyback.

To make this bulletproof, I'd want:

- A store-by-store analysis separating relocated stores from the rest, using foot traffic data by location.
- A line-by-line rebuild of expenses and a depreciation schedule.
- A few conversations with former Dick's finance or store operations people.

What would prove me wrong: the core levering expenses on a low comp, or March guidance showing real margin expansion excluding the tariff refunds.

### Variant view #2: Foot Locker is an option priced as a liability

The market values Foot Locker at about −$1.1 billion. That's only right if it burns cash forever. But its problems are concentrated in pieces that can be cut: a loss-making international business that also inflates the tax rate, $400 million a year of capex, and overlapping stores. And the people in charge are lining up to act. Stack said "simply yes" to rightsizing. The new international president, a turnaround operator from Aldi and Tesco, bought half a million dollars of stock. And the company raised a billion dollars at the bottom.

To make this bulletproof, I'd want:

- A rebuild of Foot Locker's profit by region from its old filings, since Europe's loss is the biggest unknown and isn't disclosed.
- Research on what it costs to exit European leases and labor contracts, and on licensing precedents.
- A standalone valuation of the smaller banners.
