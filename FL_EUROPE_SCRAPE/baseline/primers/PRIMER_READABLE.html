The story in five points
  1. DKS is now two businesses with very different health. The original DICK'S stores (~$14.7B of sales, ~11% profit margin) keep growing and taking share. Foot Locker (~$7.4B of sales, bought in September 2025) is losing money.
  2. The DICK'S core is growing, but the growth isn't reaching profit. Same-store sales have grown 4%+ in seven of the last eight quarters. Over the same period DKS has been spending heavily on new flagship stores, marketing and matching brand discounts, so profit has barely grown.
  3. The Foot Locker turnaround slipped by about a year. DKS cleared out Foot Locker's old inventory, cut ~30% of the shoe styles and closed 135 stores. The promised recovery at back-to-school 2026 still didn't happen. Foot Locker depends on Nike and Jordan lifestyle sneakers, which are oversupplied and discounted, and Nike says that lasts past May 2028.
  4. The 25-Aug-2026 guidance cut reset the stock. DKS cut FY26 EPS guidance from $13.50–14.50 to $11.00–12.00, and the stock fell 31% that day. It now trades at ~10x next year's consensus EPS, near its historical low.
  5. Consensus for FY27 ($13.36, +16%) leaves out several known items. It doesn't include the interest on the $1B of new bonds, any buyback with that cash, any further Foot Locker store closures, or a possible exit from Foot Locker's money-losing international business. The downside items are likely but gradual. The upside items are less certain but large. On average they roughly cancel out (Section 12).

1. How DKS makes money

Bottom line

DKS buys finished products from ~1,500 brands (Nike is ~31% of purchases) and sells them through stores, its website and app. Most of the profit comes from the DICK'S core, so two numbers matter most: how fast core sales grow, and how much of that growth turns into profit. Foot Locker is about a third of sales but currently loses money. A few small side businesses (GameChanger, advertising, own brands) carry much higher margins than the stores and are growing.

In FY25 the core had $14.1B of sales from 892 stores at an 11.1% segment margin. Foot Locker runs at ~$7.4B a year from 2,212 owned stores and loses money. On a full-year basis the combined company would have had $21.8B of sales in FY25.

1.1 Why Foot Locker earns so much less than DICK'S

Foot Locker's costs are worse on every major line. It earns a lower margin on each product, pays higher mall rents, and its small stores can't spread labor costs the way large DICK'S boxes can.

1.3 Which levers move earnings the most

The core's sales growth and margin matter most. Foot Locker margin is the second big lever. These rules of thumb are used throughout the document.

  • Core comp: each 1 point of core comp is ~$147M of sales and ~$0.33–0.42 of EPS, assuming 28–35% of the extra sales becomes profit.
  • Core margin: each 50bps of core segment margin is ~$75M of profit, or ~$0.62 of EPS. The core margin has fallen year over year in four of the last five quarters, mostly because of rising costs.
  • Foot Locker margin: on ~$7.4B of sales, each 1 point of Foot Locker margin is ~$74M, or ~$0.60 of EPS.
  • Cash: capex is ~$1.4B a year after landlord contributions, 2.3x depreciation. The $5.00 dividend costs ~$450M a year, more than the last twelve months of free cash flow ($239M). A $3.0B buyback authorization is unused, and DKS just raised $1.0B of new bonds at 6.2–6.9%.

3. Suppliers, customers and store economics

Bottom line

Nike is the supplier that matters most: ~25% of core purchases and ~59% of Foot Locker's. The DICK'S customer is suburban, family-oriented and holding up well. The Foot Locker customer is younger, lower-income, mall-based and under pressure. New House of Sport and Field House stores earn excellent returns. Foot Locker stores, especially WSS and Europe, earn poor ones.

DKS has no long-term contracts with any brand, and all brand funding is discretionary. The fastest-growing brands (On, HOKA, UGG) control their own distribution and won't fund discounts.

3.2 Customers

  • The DICK'S customer is holding up. Suburban and family-oriented. Management said every quarter from Nov-24 to May-26 that it saw "no signs of trading down." By Sep-26 it said the consumer is "under pressure" but the DICK'S customer is "holding up very, very well." DKS adds ~1.5M new loyalty members a quarter, skewing younger and more female.
  • The Foot Locker customer is under pressure. Mostly young men aged 12–25 buying sneakers as fashion, lower-income and mall-dependent. Management: this customer "may be a little bit more under pressure." A Bloomberg Intelligence survey (May-26) found 38% of households earning under $50K are waiting for sales (31% a year earlier). Exclusivity ranked low as a reason to buy, which hurts Foot Locker's launch-driven model.
  • Brands are customers too. They buy advertising on DKS's media network and pay for exclusive product and joint marketing.
  • Trade-down is uneven. Suppliers of premium goods (Under Armour, UGG) see DKS customers trading up. Suppliers of basics (workwear, denim, socks, Crocs) see smaller baskets and shoppers moving to cheaper own brands.

4.3 What management has said, topic by topic

Comps: "I don't think this is a demand issue."

Management kept the core comp guide unchanged in August. Since 1H was +5.4%, the full-year guide implies only about +1.3% for 2H. That is a big slowdown, partly because 2H laps tough comparisons (+5.7% and +3.1% last year).

Gross margin: guidance went from "up" to "down" in five months.

FY26 started at "expand," became "expand every quarter" in May, then "decline slightly" in August. The reason given: "a number of brands got very promotional on their sites," DKS matched them to stay competitive, and fuel and supply-chain costs rose. Q2's reported +79bps included ~50bps of tariff refunds and ~60bps from GameChanger and advertising, so the margin on products alone was already down ~30bps. Management expects 2H pricing to look like Q2.

Costs: the recurring bear question.

In Aug-25 management said costs would leverage at a "low single digits comp." Since then costs have grown faster than sales in six of seven quarters. Asked in Sep-26 whether flow-through "has not been awesome," the CFO answered "That's fair." Q3 FY26 is guided to ~50bps of cost deleverage, with leverage returning in Q4.

New stores: fewer than first promised.

The House of Sport target fell from "75 to 100 by end of 2027" to "67, 68," limited by the availability of top-mall locations. FY26 Field House openings were trimmed from ~22 to ~20. Opening costs are ~$90M a year, peaking at ~$36M in Q3.

Capex and cash: spending stays high.

Capex has risen from $364M (FY22) to $1,137M (FY25) to ~$1.4B net in FY26, and 2027 is "likely at the high end of $1.0–1.5B." DKS bought back no stock in Q2 FY26. On 14-Sep Stack said "We've got $1 billion on the balance sheet." Eight days later DKS raised another $1B.

Tax: up 3.5 points in six months.

The guide went 25.5% → 27% → 29%, all because Foot Locker's European losses get no tax benefit. Each point of tax is ~$0.18 of EPS.

Foot Locker: from "accretive" to "taking longer than we anticipated."

The tone went from confident to cautious in a year:

  • May-25: the deal will add to EPS in the first full year; few store closures expected.
  • Nov-25: Foot Locker "strayed from Retail 101"; DKS will "clean out the garage."
  • Mar-26: clean-out "essentially complete"; FY26 profit $100–150M with a back-to-school turnaround.
  • May-26: "Our plan is working."
  • Aug-26: guided to a loss. Asked whether DKS would shrink the fleet and sell assets: "the answer to your question is simply yes."
  • Sep-26: "It's just taking longer than we anticipated." Legacy brands hadn't supported Foot Locker as quickly as hoped. "We are creating a menu… no preconceived ideas."

2027: not guided yet.

The only framework is "drive gross margin expansion" while continuing to invest. Synergies come "a couple of years after" 2026. DKS-designed exclusives for Foot Locker arrive "to even greater degree in Q1 of next year." Discounting continues "at least through the fourth quarter."

6. Competition and pricing

Bottom line

DKS is clearly winning against other sporting goods chains and keeps gaining share. But brands set the price on most of what it sells. When Nike and other brands discounted on their own websites in summer 2026, DKS matched them, and that is what cut its gross margin. Foot Locker has even less pricing power because ~60% of what it sells is Nike, so its margin depends largely on Nike's launches and discounting.

6.2 How competition turns into pricing

DKS doesn't set the price on ~87% of what it sells.

Brands do, through minimum advertised prices and their own websites. DKS's pricing power comes from two places: its mix (own brands and firmly priced brands like On, HOKA and UGG), and its discipline in not following brands down. In Q2 FY26 it chose to follow. Stack (14-Sep-26): "we're not leading this margin erosion. We are participating in it… I use the word investment very purposely."

The discounting is concentrated in Nike lifestyle product and apparel.

Needham found over 30% of Nike's range discounted across 14 retailers (25% a year earlier). UBS data shows US wholesale Nike footwear inventory is actually lean (~27 weeks, 14 fewer than last year). The glut is in Nike apparel (~43 weeks, +12) and Nike's own websites. Deutsche Bank's July checks found retailer inventory "relatively controlled" and Nike running strong. Taken together, the problem is specific franchises, not footwear as a whole.

DKS's own promotions have deepened.

JPM tracked 70%-off clearance events in Mar–Apr 2026, "rational" promotions in May, and a "modest uptick" in August. Barclays' promotion score for DKS fell from 65 (1Q25) to 42 (1Q26).

Tariffs were a cost, then a refund, and now a tough comparison.

DKS absorbed 2025 tariffs in its margin rather than raising prices, then got $59M back after the Supreme Court ruling. Brands raised prices ~$10 on basics and up to ~$50 on top products. The refund boosted Q2 FY26 and won't repeat in FY27.

Brands have less money to fund discounts in 2027.

Nike expects its FY27 operating margin to fall, partly because it laps its own $986M tariff refund, so it has less room to help retailers with markdowns. Firmly priced brands give no markdown money. Suppliers said that in Jun–Jul DKS hadn't asked for extra markdown money except to clear World Cup product.

Where it lands.

DKS has pricing power in running, own brands and exclusives. It has little in Nike/Jordan lifestyle, where Nike sets both the wholesale and the retail price and is currently clearing stock. Foot Locker is ~60% Nike, so its margin follows Nike's launch calendar and discounting. That, more than anything DKS can control, is why the Foot Locker recovery slipped.

7.4 What it should trade at

The market is treating Foot Locker as a liability.

Value the core on its own: FY27 consensus core profit of $1,647M, less ~$137M of interest (including the new bonds), plus ~$25M of other income, taxed at ~25% gives ~$1,150M, or ~$12.90 per share. At the 12–13x DICK'S traded at before the deal, that is $155–168 per share. At $137, the market is implicitly valuing Foot Locker at about −$12 to −$17 per share (−$1.1 to −$1.5B).

A fair multiple for the core is 12–13x.

In its favor: ~9% market share gaining ~0.5 points a year, 4–6% comps, a margin that has structurally risen from ~5% before COVID to ~11%, and new stores with 20–40% cash returns. Against it: heavy capex, profit that hasn't grown with sales for a year, Nike at ~25% of purchases, and a choice to match brand discounts. Specialty retailers trade at ~12x. 15x would need proof of the 2027 recovery.

Our fair value range.

  • Base ~$155–165: 12–13x core EPS of ~$12.90, with Foot Locker valued at zero.
  • Bull ~$195–210: core at 14x on $13.50–14.00 as costs finally leverage, plus Foot Locker reaching a 2–3% margin by FY28 (worth $1.50–2.50 of EPS).
  • Bear ~$100–110: core margin slips to ~10% on a 1–2% comp, Foot Locker loses $100M+ again in FY27, tax stays at 29%. EPS of ~$10–11 at 10x.
  • Weighted: roughly $150–160, before two possible events (a buyback funded by the new bonds, an international exit). Each could add $0.50–1.20 to FY27 EPS (Section 12).

Why the discount persists.

The multiple fell from ~15x to ~10x in one print because the market lost faith in two things at once: that the core margin had been permanently reset at 11–12% (DKS cut it to 10.6–10.9% despite a 4.9% comp), and that Foot Locker could be fixed with "Retail 101" (DKS guided to a loss just 90 days after raising). Only results will fix that. The next test is 24-Nov-26.

10. Foot Locker deep dive

Bottom line

Foot Locker is struggling for five reasons: too dependent on Nike, too many small mall stores, a business built on sneaker launches that stopped selling out, weak operations, and acquisitions that didn't work. DKS has fixed the operational problems quickly but can't fix the demand problem. The new strategy (fewer styles, more running brands, apparel back in) points in the right direction, but brands like On and HOKA control how fast Foot Locker can get their product. There are many signs DKS may exit or shrink the money-losing international business, and none of it is in consensus.

10.1 The deal

DKS bought Foot Locker cheaply because it was in bad shape. DKS paid ~$2.5B ($24 per share, mostly in DKS stock) for ~$8B of sales and ~$395M of FY24 adjusted EBITDA: about 6x EBITDA and 0.3x sales. DKS had walked away in Nov-2024 after diligence showed the turnaround was bigger than expected, then came back at a price 40% below its earlier $40 offer. Foot Locker's board accepted because its standalone plan had become unreachable (weak Nike, weak international, tariffs). DKS promised the deal would add to EPS in the first full year. Instead FY26 is guided to a $40–80M loss on 9.6M extra shares.

Operating margin fell from ~12% at the FY21 peak to slightly negative by 2025, as Nike pulled back its best product and costs kept rising. Store count fell 24% but total square footage only 3%.

10.3 Why Foot Locker is in trouble: five causes

1. It depends on Nike, and Nike cut back its best product.

Nike was 75% of purchases at the FY20 peak. From 2021 to 2023 Nike shifted to selling directly to consumers and cut what it sent to Foot Locker. The biggest loss was "high-heat" product, the scarce launches that sell out at full price.

  • Foot Locker's share of Nike's hottest product fell from ~40% to ~20% (Wells estimate).
  • Foot Locker's sales of that product fell from $1.6B (FY21) to $0.7B (FY23).
  • Wells attributes ~5 of the ~9 points of margin lost to Nike.
  • Stack's diagnosis: Foot Locker "did not react quickly enough when its largest brand pivoted toward a direct-to-consumer model, leaving Foot Locker with the wrong inventory."

2. Too many small stores in malls.

A typical US Foot Locker has ~3,500 sq ft of selling space. Rent is 13.3% of sales versus 8.5% at DICK'S. Over 75% of Champs stores are within a mile of a Foot Locker, often in the same mall. Foot Locker's own 2023 plan called for closing 400+ weaker mall stores. About 35% of its mall stores are in lower-quality malls (Wells).

3. Its model relied on launches that stopped selling out.

Footwear is 84% of sales, mostly bought by young men as fashion. Stack says Foot Locker was "too dependent" on Jordan Retro, "pretty much missed" the retro running trend, and let apparel deteriorate. In summer 2026 the launches themselves failed. None of July's six retro Jordans resold above retail, and 7 of 10 in August sold at a discount (Needham).

4. Weak operations ("Retail 101").

Inventory rose 37% in FY21 and 30% in FY22 while sales fell. There was no planning or replenishment team, Foot Locker had three CFOs in fifteen months, and no outdoor advertising since ~2013. Operating costs rose from 20.6% of sales (FY19) to 25.4% (1H25).

5. Acquisitions and diversification that failed.

WSS (bought 2021 for $809M) was meant to reach 300+ stores and ~$1.3B. It peaked at 151 stores and $660M, has comped negative since Jan-2023, and is now down to 99 stores. atmos (bought for $372M) has been written down twice. Champs fell from $1.9B to $1.2B of sales. Foot Locker also exited or licensed out nine countries before the deal (Singapore, Malaysia, Greece, Romania and others).

10.5 The product shift, and whether consumer data support it

What DKS is changing at Foot Locker:

  • Fewer styles. ~30% of shoe styles cut in Fast Break stores. A Deckers executive described it as "edit to amplify": the cut isn't meant to lower sales, and margins are "much stronger." Stack says there are still "too many SKUs."
  • Less reliance on launches and retro. The plan is to broaden into running and other brands (On, HOKA, Saucony, Salomon, ASICS, UGG, Birkenstock, Nike running), the same shift that worked at DICK'S.
  • Apparel back in. Foot Locker had largely walked away from national-brand apparel. DKS wants to "dress the kid toe to head."
  • DKS doing the buying. Product through Q2 FY26 was bought by prior management. Back-to-school 2026 was the first season fully bought by DKS, and DKS-designed exclusives arrive mostly in Q1 FY27.
  • Inventory is rising again. ~$1.5B in January, ~$2.0B in August, against a −3.6% comp.

The direction is right, but timing is against it. Running demand is slowing, the running brands are limiting supply, and Foot Locker's legacy Nike business is deteriorating faster than it can shift away.

10.6 International: is an exit coming?

The business. Foot Locker International (Europe, Asia Pacific, Canada) is ~$2.2B a year, ~10% of DKS sales and roughly a third of Foot Locker. Its comps have been negative six quarters in a row under two owners. DKS doesn't disclose its profit, but every available proxy points to losses: Foot Locker's own international pre-tax result went from +$84M (FY22) to −$49M (FY24), and a full tax reserve covers $280M of foreign tax losses because management sees no "prudent and feasible business plans for generating future taxable income" there.

Signs pointing toward an exit, licensing deal or deep cuts

  1. Management's tone has turned. Dec-25: "we're keeping that." Since then: Europe "roughly six months behind," then "more challenging than expected," then hurt by the Middle East war. "Keeping" hasn't been repeated.
  2. Stack has said yes to restructuring in plain terms. Asked about shrinking the fleet and selling Foot Locker assets: "the answer to your question is simply yes." In September: "we are creating a menu… no preconceived ideas."
  3. The tax reserve. A full valuation allowance means DKS's own forecasts show no path to profit in those entities. It costs ~4 points of tax, ~$0.65 per share.
  4. Foot Locker's own board planned to pull back. Its pre-deal case moved capital away from international toward the more profitable US banners.
  5. There's a template. Foot Locker already converted Singapore, Malaysia, Greece and Romania to licensed partners, and licensed stores keep growing (246 → 266).
  6. There's budget left. ~$234M of the $750M charge program is unused, and the 10-K flags more closure costs at Foot Locker.
  7. The hire fits a restructuring. The new International head came from Aldi and Tesco, a cost-cutting background rather than a sneaker one.
  8. The new bonds make it easier. Their terms exclude non-US subsidiaries from key covenants, which removes a hurdle to selling them.
  9. Nike doesn't name Foot Locker as a key European partner. It names JD Sports, Sports Direct and Snipes.

What argues against a near-term exit

  • International was part of the deal's pitch ("will make DICK'S a global business"), and Fast Break is being tested in Europe.
  • Europe was Foot Locker's best region as recently as FY24 (+4.4% comp).
  • Exits are expensive and fetch little: European labor councils, 5–10 year leases, a new Dutch distribution center, and past sales raised only $12–24M each. Selling at a loss would also trigger write-downs of the $591M of goodwill and $710M of brand value.
  • The natural buyer, JD Sports, is cutting guidance itself.
  • The International head just bought $500K of stock, which is unusual for someone planning to close his own division.

Is it in the estimates? No.

Bloomberg keeps Foot Locker Europe at ~550 stores through FY32, has Foot Locker revenue growing from FY28, and assumes the tax rate drifts down to 25% by FY29 without saying how. No broker models an exit.

An exit would cut revenue ~10% but lift EPS by roughly $0.90–1.30 (7–10%), mainly by removing losses and lowering the tax rate.

11.2 What the important calls said

Nike planning manager, Americas (14-Jul) ★★★★★

Bottom line: Nike and DKS are working closely together again, and Foot Locker loaded DKS with extra inventory.

  • The Nike relationship is now a problem for other brands. When Nike pulled back from wholesale a few years ago, HOKA and On got space at DKS. Now Nike is reinvesting in DKS with joint marketing, store displays and DKS-only colorways. He called this "the biggest elephant in the room" for competing brands.
  • Foot Locker increased DKS's inventory. DKS is working it down through returns to vendors, smaller orders and off-price channels. He saw the heavy inventory as a positive. The August results showed it wasn't.
  • Money is going to running. Spending is concentrated on the running wall ("super shoes from all brands"). DKS is cutting lifestyle and niche categories (training, tennis, soccer after the World Cup) and is slow to reorder when trends change.
  • Foot Locker will stay Nike-heavy. Brands are "excited" about Fast Break, but he expects Foot Locker to be "very Nike-forward moving forward." That contradicts management's story that Foot Locker will broaden into On, HOKA and Saucony.
  • Caveat: his 2026 store-opening figures (10–15 House of Sport, 18–25 Field House) are higher than the company's plan (~14 and ~20).

Jordan Brand merchandising director, North America (13-Jul) ★★★★★

Bottom line: He said Jordan Retro was a "bright spot" at Foot Locker. Six weeks later management said launches underperformed.

  • Jordan has a multi-year plan with DKS. There is a formal "four- to five-year plan" across DICK'S and Foot Locker. House of Sport gets the most exclusive product; regular DICK'S customers won't pay $180 for a shoe.
  • Why his miss matters most. The brand that controls Foot Locker's best product didn't see the Q2 miss coming. That suggests the problem is weak consumer demand (few launches reselling above retail, Jordan spreading to Famous Footwear and Academy), not Foot Locker getting too little product.
  • Pricing friction is minor. The only dispute he mentioned was over customization charges ($5 versus $10–15 per item).

Under Armour merchandising director, Americas (10-Jul) ★★★★★

Bottom line: The best read on the core business, and the only call that predicted the discounting.

  • Premium demand is solid. The DKS customer keeps "buying more of the premium products," with spending concentrated around big moments (Father's Day, back-to-school). Base layers grew double digits for three years running.
  • Discounting was coming. "DICK'S and the marketplace at large are definitely getting more promotional." Vendor terms were unchanged, so the cost falls on DKS's gross margin. Premium fleece at $120–150 was the weak spot.
  • DKS is cutting brands. DKS now picks "one brand to stand for something" in each slot (one cotton hoodie brand, one running-short brand). This is how mid-tier brands lose space to DKS's own brands.
  • The relationship is warming. Stack visited UA's campus, and "40+" DKS buyers and executives visited UA's offices. On Foot Locker: no effect on UA's buys, and a stronger Foot Locker "lifts all boats."

Nike-side planner, 24 years (6-Jul) ★★★★

Bottom line: DKS plus Foot Locker will buy less in total but negotiate harder. That is good for DKS's margin over time and bad for Foot Locker's sales.

  • Demand split. Running and retro running are doing "incredibly well"; lifestyle sportswear "continues to be a challenge." Shoppers are also buying casual brands like Birkenstock.
  • Less volume. "The two combined will purchase less than historically they have than when they were on their own," because of overlap and store closures.
  • More bargaining power. Owning Foot Locker "starts to eliminate who you can use as leverage," so brands want JD Sports to grow in the US as a counterweight.

Deckers / UGG, two interviews (28 and 29-Jul) ★★★★

Bottom line: UGG is a big winner at DKS. The view that Foot Locker's style cuts wouldn't hurt sales was wrong.

  • UGG at DKS is booming. Shipments +238% in May–Jul and ~+150% for fall, mostly deeper allocation in existing stores (largely House of Sport). Sell-through matches sell-in, and DKS respects UGG's minimum prices.
  • The case for Fast Break. The senior director called it "edit to amplify": a 20–30% style cut that "isn't dropping revenue expectations" and gives "much stronger margin." Foot Locker's −3.6% comp and rising inventory contradict this.
  • Caveat: her claim that DKS is "about 18%" of UGG's North American revenue looks too high; it would make DKS a ~$900M UGG account.

Crocs / HEYDUDE (6-Jul) ★★★

Bottom line: The clearest picture of who DKS takes share from and who it could lose to.

  • Taking share from: Academy (the "closest direct competitor"), Hibbett, Kohl's, JCPenney, Macy's and regional chains that can't keep up on assortment, digital and delivery.
  • Could lose share to: SCHEELS (high service, growing), Amazon and marketplaces, Fanatics, and off-price if excess inventory floods the market.
  • Consumers are splitting. Shoppers are "very price sensitive": they either go to value channels or to top retailers for top products, cutting out the middle.
  • Own brands replace mid-tier brands. At Reebok, DKS removed all Reebok apparel from stores because it competed with DKS's own label.

Workwear planner (10-Jul) ★★★

Bottom line: Big brands are losing pull, which helps DKS's own brands, and DKS was pulling orders forward.

  • "Nike, North Face, Patagonia don't have the brand power that they used to," which opens space for smaller brands and private label.
  • DKS was "trying to front-load next season as much as possible" with "aggressive" reorders, consistent with the inventory build.
  • His own forecast accuracy is "probably being low."

12.E The long and short cases

The long case

  1. DKS announces concrete Foot Locker action at Q3 or in March (Europe, Champs/WSS, closures, capex cuts). That removes losses, a 4-point tax drag and $0.2B+ of capex from a business the market already values at −$1.1 to −1.5B.
  2. The first DKS-bought assortments and ~350 Fast Break stores deliver a positive Foot Locker comp and a 28%+ gross margin by Q1 FY27.
  3. The core gross margin recovers as Nike's channel clears in early 2027, with new stores, own brands and GameChanger adding 30–50bps.
  4. $1B of fresh cash and a $3.0B authorization at ~10x earnings, with insiders buying.
  5. A core that just comped +4.9% (versus Academy ~+1% and JD −7%) trades at ~12x on its own, so Foot Locker comes free.

What it needs: a Q3 Foot Locker comp no worse than −2.5%, Foot Locker gross margin no lower than 24%, and management announcing "the menu."

The short case

  1. Foot Locker is ~60% Nike, and Nike says its lifestyle/Jordan discounting runs past DKS's FY27. Jordan is now also sold at Famous Footwear and Academy.
  2. The core laps +6.0% and +4.9% in 1H FY27 with high gas prices and no World Cup, while the 2H FY26 guide already implies ~+1%.
  3. Inventory is up 63.5% y/y going into a holiday management calls promotional "at least through Q4."
  4. $66M of new interest and a 29% tax rate aren't in consensus, yet consensus FY27 EPS still implies +16% growth.
  5. The turnaround is "early," synergies are "none yet," and the only Foot Locker beats came in the quarters when its inventory was being cleared out.

What it needs: a Q3 Foot Locker comp below −3%, a Q3 core comp below +1%, and no structural announcement.
